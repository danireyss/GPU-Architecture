The big idea: the host link is 20 times narrower, so copy once and keep the data on the GPU.

  • The board uses 25 GB/s and the check question 20 GB/s. The 8 GB copy runs at 25. The training and kernel examples run at 20, so the pipe is drawn thinner there and is 25 times narrower than VRAM, not 20.
  • Full-width PCIe 3, 4 and 5 carry about 16, 32 and 64 GB/s, and real copies get somewhat less.
  • The extra hop from ordinary RAM is shown without a cost, because it varies by system. The timeline does not change between steps 1 and 2.
  • The three-kernel example assumes each kernel's output is also 2 GB.
Where the numbers come from
  • The model: one pure function, written and tested before drawing. Copy time = bytes ÷ link speed, and a step takes copy + compute in sequence, or the longer of the two when they overlap. Every bar, level, readout and chip comes from it.
  • PCIe figures: a full-width link is 16 lanes. PCIe 3 runs 8 gigatransfers a second per lane with 128b/130b coding, so 8 × 16 × 128/130 ÷ 8 = 15.75 GB/s one way. PCIe 4 and 5 double that each time, to 31.5 and 63. Packet overheads take more off, which is why real copies get less.
  • The course's example chip: 500 GB/s of VRAM and 20 trillion operations a second are example figures, not one real product. "About 6 trillion" is 6.4 trillion rounded, and it assumes the lanes could have been fully busy for all 0.32 s.
  • Overlapping: the 0.4 s a step holds once the run is under way. The first batch still takes 0.5 s, because nothing has been copied ahead of it. On the timeline, three batches take 1.3 s against 1.5 s. The 25% is the steady rate, 2.5 against 2 batches a second. It needs an asynchronous copy, and a real one is rarely this cleanly timed.
  • Keeping it on the GPU saves 0.4 s of 1.8 s because 4 of the 6 copies disappear. With one kernel there is nothing to save, and "New job" shows that case.
  • Pinned memory: pageable memory can be moved by the operating system, so the driver copies it into a pinned staging buffer before the GPU's copy engine reads it. Allocating pinned memory skips that hop. This is from my knowledge of NVIDIA's CUDA guidance and PyTorch's pin_memory option, and I did not recheck it for this page.
  • New job sizes and computing times are round figures chosen for the figure, on the real PCIe speeds above. They assume each kernel's output is the same size as its input.
  • Drawing choices: the VRAM pipe's dashes run while the chip computes, to show it working from VRAM, but the model does not use that rate for computing. The clock runs 4 to 8 times slower than real time, and the rate is what matters, not the speed of the dashes on screen. The pipe's dashes do scale with the link's rate.
  • Left out: latency and setup for each copy, the time to allocate memory, and a machine with NVLink or shared memory, where the host link can be much wider.
