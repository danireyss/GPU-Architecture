The big idea: one instruction works on every lane at once.

Where the numbers come from
  • 512 / 32 = 16 lanes, 32 zmm registers and the mask registers k0–k7 (k1–k7 usable as write masks) are AVX-512 as Intel documents it in the Intel 64 and IA-32 Architectures Software Developer's Manual. vpaddd adds 16 packed 32-bit integers, and {k1}{z} zeroes the lanes the mask turns off.
  • 1,000 elements is just the example. The rest is arithmetic: 1,000 = 62 × 16 + 8, so 63 vector adds, and 1,000 / (63 × 16) = 99.2% lane use. At 128 bits it's 250 adds, and at 256 bits 125, with nothing left over.
  • The figure counts add instructions only. Each step also needs two loads and a store (the scalar loop does too), and the loop counter and branch are left out.
  • Speedup: the add count drops 15.9×. Real loops gain less than the lane count, because a loop like this spends most of its time waiting on memory, not adding. Some CPUs also run 512-bit instructions at a lower clock or split them into two 256-bit halves.
  • Memory is drawn as three strips for clarity. A real load passes through L2 and L3 on its way to L1, and the figure skips them.
