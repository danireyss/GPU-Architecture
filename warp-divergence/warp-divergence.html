The big idea: a warp takes every path that any of its threads takes.

Where the numbers come from
  • 32 threads per warp is real for NVIDIA GPUs (CUDA C++ Programming Guide). AMD GPUs run wavefronts of 64 or 32.
  • The slot costs are illustrative: compare 1, sqrt 4, y = 0 is 1, z = y + 1 is 1, and the branch bookkeeping is free. Real costs depend on the GPU: sqrt runs on a special-function unit with fewer lanes than the main ALUs, and branches cost something.
  • A branch this short is usually not a branch at all. Compilers normally turn it into a select (or predicated instructions): every lane computes both sides and keeps one, with no jump and no mask stack. The figure uses a real branch so you can watch the mask work.
  • The mask stack is the classic textbook model of how GPUs before Volta reconverged (a "SIMT stack" of mask, next PC and rejoin PC). Volta and later NVIDIA GPUs track each thread's own PC and rejoin at convergence barriers, but a warp still issues one instruction for all its active lanes, so the slot counts come out the same.
  • The arithmetic: diverged 1 + 4 + 1 + 1 = 7 slots and 32×1 + 16×4 + 16×1 + 32×1 = 144 useful lane-slots of 7 × 32 = 224, which is 64.3%. All agree: 6 slots, 192 of 192. Sorting two mixed warps into one all-true and one all-false warp: 7 + 7 = 14 becomes 6 + 3 = 9. Lopsided loop: 31 × 10 + 1,000 = 1,310 useful lane-iterations of 32 × 1,000 = 32,000, so 95.9% wasted.
  • Every y and z on the lane displays is computed in the page from that lane's x. The x values are random (positive where the pattern says true, zero or negative where it says false), and "New warp" draws new ones.
