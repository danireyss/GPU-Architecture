The big idea: while one warp waits for memory, another one computes.

Where the numbers come from
  • 65,536 registers per SM and 64 resident warps per SM are real limits, from NVIDIA's CUDA C++ Programming Guide (the per-compute-capability table) for data-centre parts: V100 (7.0), A100 (8.0), H100 (9.0). Consumer Ampere and Ada (8.6, 8.9) cap at 48 warps, not 64.
  • 1,024 registers per warp is 32 threads × 32 registers per thread. That's a choice made for this kernel, not a hardware constant. At 32 registers per thread the register file and the warp cap both run out at 64 warps.
  • 10-cycle compute bursts and the 100-cycle VRAM wait are teaching numbers, chosen so the sums come out round. Real DRAM latency on recent NVIDIA GPUs is several hundred core cycles; microbenchmark studies such as Jia et al., Dissecting the NVIDIA Volta GPU Architecture via Microbenchmarking (2018), measure around 400 or more. The 300-cycle step is closer to real hardware.
  • One issue port feeding 32 lanes is a simplification. A real SM has four schedulers, one per sub-partition, but the reasoning is the same for each.
  • Busy share = min(1, N × 10 / (10 + W)), and you need 1 + W/10 warps to cover the wait (Little's law). The gauge and the displays don't show this formula. They show a cycle-by-cycle simulation of the same machine, measured over the last 10 + W cycles, and in steady state it lands on exactly the formula's numbers.
